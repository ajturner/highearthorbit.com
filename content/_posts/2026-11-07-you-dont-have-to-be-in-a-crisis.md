In 2012, Government Technology named Heather Blanchard, Noel Dickover and me to its [Top 25 Doers, Dreamers and Drivers](https://www.govtech.com/top-25/CrisisCommons-Co-Founders.html) for co-founding CrisisCommons. I recently reread the piece. It is short, and it gets the arc right. What it can't hold is what those years meant, and how much of my work since then grew out of them.

## How it started

We met at Transparency Camp in 2009, an unconference about open government. The question we kept coming back to was simple: people who build software and maps want to help when disaster strikes, but there is no obvious place for them to show up. Money is easy to give. Skills are not.

So we asked. That June, the first CrisisCamp brought about 200 people together in Washington to talk through what volunteer technologists could do, and how to do it without getting in the way of the people already doing the response work.

## Haiti

Then on January 12, 2010, the earthquake struck Haiti, and the question stopped being theoretical. Within days we had [CrisisCamps running in five cities](/articles/grassroots-crisis-development-organization/) with more than 400 people working on over a dozen projects. The week after, there were fourteen more camps. Within fifteen weeks there were 65 camps in ten countries.

The work was concrete. Volunteers built a translation app so responders could communicate. Others traced roads and buildings into OpenStreetMap from fresh satellite imagery, giving Port-au-Prince a usable damage map where good maps barely existed before. We loaded maps and data onto [USB drives](/articles/updates-from-haiti/) and sent them down for teams working with little or no connectivity.

What struck me then, and still does, is that technology multiplies a contribution. A donated hour of labor is used once. A dataset, a map tile or a piece of code shared openly feeds every other project that needs it.

## Becoming a bridge

The hardest part was never the code. Traditional responders were rightly wary of a crowd of enthusiastic volunteers arriving with untested tools. The real job became translation in both directions: helping response organizations say what they actually needed, and helping volunteers build that instead of what was fun to build. That is what GovTech meant by CrisisCommons becoming "a liaison between response organizations and technical volunteers." With support from the [Sloan Foundation](/articles/crisiscommons-receives-funding-from-sloan-foundation/), we spent the next two years trying to make that bridge durable.

In January 2012, Heather and Noel stepped down so the organization could grow beyond its founders, which takes its own kind of leadership. I stayed on while serving as CTO of GeoIQ (then FortiusOne). Later that year, during Hurricane Sandy, I sat in FEMA's National Response Coordination Center alongside FEMA, White House and Red Cross staff while [CrisisCamps in more than ten cities](/articles/crisiscamp-sandy/) worked remotely on gas station availability, damage reports and getting kids back to school. Crowdsourcing was no longer the outsider's idea. It was assumed.

## What it means to me now

Noel said it best in the article: CrisisCommons gave people "the idea that you don't have to be in a crisis to make a difference."

That idea is the thread through everything I have worked on since. A disaster exposes, all at once, whether a community's information is open, current and usable by anyone who needs it. Most of the time those gaps are invisible. The lesson of CrisisCommons was that the best preparation for a crisis is ordinary, everyday infrastructure: governments publishing authoritative data openly, and communities who know how to use it. That is the work behind ArcGIS Hub, and why I still think of open data as public infrastructure, as basic as roads.
